How mobile blasting works

No jargon. What actually happens when we turn up, how the media gets chosen, and why the surface underneath matters more than the paint that goes over it.

The short version

Compressed air, abrasive, and a lot of masking

Blasting fires abrasive at a surface hard enough to knock off whatever is stuck to it — rust, paint, scale, grime — and leaves clean, textured metal behind. The mobile part means the compressor, pot and containment arrive on a trailer, so nothing has to be hauled anywhere.

Step by step

What a job actually looks like

Five stages. The blasting itself is rarely the longest one.

STEP 01

We walk the job

Before anything gets quoted we look at what it is, what is on it, and what has to be protected. Media and pressure get chosen here — from the substrate first, then the coating. This is also where lead paint gets flagged, because pre-1978 coatings change the containment and the price.

STEP 02

Mask and contain

Usually the longest part of the day and the part that separates a clean job from an expensive mistake. Cylinders, seals, bearings, glass, decals and electrical connectors get masked. Tarps and screens go up around the work zone so nothing drifts onto your inventory, your neighbour's car or the water.

STEP 03

Blast

Compressed air drives abrasive through the nozzle at 90–110 PSI. Water injected at the tip knocks the dust down and keeps the surface cool. The operator holds a consistent standoff and angle — too close gouges, too far wastes air and media.

STEP 04

Rinse and inhibit

Spent media gets collected and the surface rinsed. On wet jobs a holding inhibitor buys time before flash rust starts, because bare steel in Michigan humidity begins oxidising within hours, not days.

STEP 05

Coat, same day

Prepared steel has a shelf life measured in hours. We schedule so your painter is ready and the primer goes on inside the window. Prep that sits overnight in a humid shop is prep that gets done twice.

The bit nobody explains

Why the profile matters

Blasting is not only cleaning. It cuts a microscopic angular tooth into the steel, and that tooth is what a coating grips. Too shallow and paint peels. Too deep and the peaks telegraph through a thin film. Coating data sheets specify this window in mils — it is the number your warranty depends on.

This is also why painting over rust always fails. Primer bonded to loose oxide is bonded to something already coming off. More on rust removal →

Choosing media

Not all abrasive is the same

Media gets picked for the substrate first, then checked against the coating. Choose for the coating alone and you strip the paint and ruin the part. And never silica sand — that is what causes silicosis, and it is why the trade moved on.

Crushed glass

The everyday default

Recycled bottle glass. Light, sharp and cheap, so it cuts paint and light-to-moderate rust efficiently without hitting the substrate as hard as garnet. Silica-free. This is what most of your job will run unless something argues otherwise.

30–60 mesh
Profile
1.5–2.5 mil
Hardness
Moderate (5.5 Mohs)

Garnet

When there's real tonnage to remove

Denser and harder, so every particle lands with more energy. Cuts stubborn coatings faster and leaves a deeper, sharper profile. Recyclable through several passes, which offsets the higher price on big jobs.

Size
30–60 mesh
Profile
2–3.5 mil
Hardness
Hard (7.5–8 Mohs)
Heavy scale, two-part epoxy, structural steel to spec
Thin sheet metal, decorative surfaces

Soda

Strips without touching the metal

Sodium bicarbonate breaks up on impact, so it lifts coating and leaves the substrate essentially untouched. That is exactly what you want on a boat hull or an aluminium panel — and exactly wrong if the surface has to be primed, because there is no tooth for the coating to grip.

Profile

Very soft (2.5 Mohs)

Wet or dry

Water at the nozzle changes everything

Injecting water wets the abrasive so it stays heavy instead of becoming a dust plume. That is what makes it possible to work in an occupied shop, at a marina, or in a subdivision. It also cools the surface, which is why thin sheet metal goes to wet blasting.

The trade-off: wet-blasted bare steel starts flash rusting within hours in Michigan humidity. That is chemistry, not a defect — it just means the primer has to go on the same day. How we plan around flash rust →

Pricing

What actually drives the number

Six things, roughly in order of impact.

Surface area

The main driver. Square footage of actual surface, not the footprint of the object.

What's on it

Light surface rust is fast. Ten layers of two-part epoxy or thick mill scale is not.

Masking complexity

A bare frame is quick. A machine with cylinders, seals, glass and decals takes hours before blasting starts.

Access

Whether the rig can get within about 150 feet, and whether we're working at height or in a confined space.

Containment

An open farm yard is straightforward. Working beside a running production line or over water is not.

Lead paint

Pre-1978 coatings require full containment and regulated waste disposal. It is a real cost and it gets raised up front.
